Why the sky is blue

mockup · a leaf whose reveal is a 3-page nested sheet

Recall

  • Sunlight is broadband white; air molecules scatter it.
  • Rayleigh regime: intensity ∝ 1/λ⁴.
  • 450nm scatters ~5x more than 650nm.
  • Violet scatters harder still — and we still see blue.

Self-test

Sunlight carries every visible wavelength. Explain why the sky we look at is blue rather than violet — and where else the same mechanism shows up.

Reveal answer

1 · The mechanism

  • Air molecules are far smaller than λ — the Rayleigh regime.
  • Scattered intensity ∝ 1/λ⁴, so λ dominates everything else.
  • 450nm vs 650nm: (650/450)⁴ ≈ 4.4–5.5x more blue redirected.
  • White light comes in; blue is what gets steered at your eye.
  • So the sky's colour is borrowed sunlight, not emitted light.

2 · Why not violet

  • Violet (~400nm) scatters harder than blue (~450nm).
  • But the Sun puts out less violet than blue to begin with.
  • Ozone absorbs a slice of the violet band on the way in.
  • Our S-cones are far weaker at 400nm than at 480nm.
  • The eye sums that short-wavelength mix and reports blue.

3 · Where else it shows

  • Sunset: a longer path scatters the blue out — red survives.
  • Clouds are white: Mie scattering is wavelength-independent.
  • From orbit there is no air, so there is nothing to scatter.
  • Snow in shade reads blue — lit only by the sky.
  • Direct sunlight is the blue subtracted: warm, or yellow.
